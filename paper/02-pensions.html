Pensions

Pension systems hold an important place within the welfare state, as they play a decisive role in shaping income distribution among the population (Breznau, 2023; Esping-Andersen, 1990). Although these systems are based on the shared premise that older adults require financial support after retirement, the principles of justice that guide their design vary widely across societies. These differences shape how individuals conceive of fairness and evaluate the legitimacy of benefit distribution (Liebig & Sauer, 2016). As noted above, in contexts where ideals of social solidarity predominate, redistributive mechanisms allow for a more equitable distribution among different social groups. In contrast, in societies where meritocratic beliefs are stronger, inequality in pensions tends to be justified on the basis of work history, individual contributions, or personal effort (Busemeyer, 2013; Castillo et al., 2024; Lindh, 2015; Mijs, 2019). Understanding these principles of justice—and their influence on support for different pension arrangements—is essential for analysing the legitimacy of welfare.

Despite its sustained economic growth during the 1990s, Chile remains one of the most unequal countries in Latin America and the OECD (Llorca-Jaña & Miller, 2021). This paradox reflects the inability of growth to translate into a more equitable distribution of its benefits. According to specialist sources, the poorest 50% of the population earns just 10% of total income and has negative net wealth, while the richest 1% receives almost 27% of income and controls approximately half of the national wealth (Chancel et al., 2022). These inequalities are not solely the result of market dynamics, but are rooted in neoliberal reforms that privatised and commodified central areas of social reproduction (Arrizabalo Montoro et al., 2019; Ferre, 2023).

Neoliberal reforms introduced market logic into essential public services through concessions and regulatory frameworks designed to favour the private sector, consolidating what authors such as Madriaga (Madariaga, 2020) have identified as a model of commodification of public services that has served as a pillar of the neoliberal order. This disproportionately benefits higher-income groups, leaving lower-income sectors dependent on more limited public alternatives. Systems such as health and education have been studied in depth in this regard (Llambías-Wolff, 2019; Rosenzvaig-Hernandez, 2024), revealing the existence of a structurally segmented system where access and quality depend largely on the ability to pay (Castillo et al., 2025).

The Chilean pension system is one of the clearest examples of this market-oriented approach. Based on individual capitalisation with mandatory contributions administered by private entities (AFPs), it produces significant inequalities: those who have had higher incomes throughout their working lives receive considerably higher pensions, while workers with low incomes or unstable employment histories face insufficient benefits (Bril-Mascarenhas & Maillet, 2019; Evans & Pienknagura, 2024). Although 11 million people currently participate in the AFPs, according to data from the Superintendency of Pensions (Superintendencia, 2025), about 27% of the workforce remains outside the system due to informality. This structure has contributed to the high incidence of poverty in old age, generating deep social unrest and movements demanding substantive reforms (Rozas Bugueño & Maillet, 2019). Paradoxically, however, recent studies show that a growing proportion of the population tends to justify pension inequalities on the basis of individual contributions (Castillo et al., 2025).

This scenario reflects broader transformations in class structure and moral orientations in Chile. Meritocratic beliefs have intensified, accompanying the internalisation of market-based moral frameworks in which individuals and families conceive of themselves as agents responsible for maximising their own market value (Canales Cerón et al., 2021; Mau, 2015). In a context marked by persistent inequalities and the growing entrenchment of meritocratic beliefs, it is crucial to examine how pension inequalities are justified, how these justifications vary according to social class and meritocratic orientations, and how they have evolved in recent years (Castillo et al., 2025; Castillo et al., 2024).
