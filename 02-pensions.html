Inequality in Pensions and Social Services

Pension systems occupy a central position within the welfare state, playing a crucial role in shaping income distribution among population (Breznau, 2023; Esping-Andersen, 1990). These systems rest on a shared understanding that older adults require financial support after retirement. However, the principles of justice underlying pension system design vary considerably across societies, influencing how individuals perceive fairness and the legitimacy of benefit distribution (Liebig & Sauer, 2016). In systems emphasizing social solidarity, redistributive mechanisms ensure more equitable pension distribution across different social groups, while in societies where meritocratic beliefs predominate, individuals tend to justify pension inequalities based on factors such as work history, contributions to the system, and individual effort (Castillo et al., 2024; Mijs, 2019). Understanding these justice principles and how they shape support for different pension arrangements becomes essential for analyzing welfare legitimacy and social cohesion.

Despite experiencing sustained economic growth since the 1990s that significantly reduced income poverty, Chile remains one of the most unequal countries in Latin America and the OECD (Chancel et al., 2022; Llorca-Jaña & Miller, 2021). This paradox reflects how economic growth failed to translate into equitable distribution of its benefits. The poorest 50% of the population captures merely 10% of total income and holds negative net wealth, while the wealthiest 1% receives nearly 27% of income and controls approximately half of national wealth (Chancel et al., 2022). These extreme disparities are not solely the product of market dynamics, but are deeply rooted in neoliberal reforms that privatized and commodified key domains of social reproduction since the military dictatorship (Arrizabalo Montoro et al., 2019; Ferre, 2023).

Neoliberal reforms introduced market logic into essential public services through concessions, subsidies, vouchers, and pro-private regulatory frameworks, creating what scholars term a model of “public-service capitalism” (Boccardo, 2020; Madariaga, 2020). This model disproportionately benefits higher-income groups, leaving lower-income populations dependent on limited public options. The health sector illustrates this segmentation: approximately 79% of the population is covered by the public insurer FONASA, while 15% are enrolled in private ISAPREs offering faster and higher-quality services (MIDEPLAN, 2024). In education, only 30.6% of students attend fully public schools, while 54% access voucher-funded institutions and 9.3% attend fully private schools serving primarily affluent families (Mineduc, 2023). This arrangement has created a structurally segmented system where access and quality depend on ability to pay, reinforced by state subsidies to private providers (PNUD, 2017).

The Chilean pension system particularly exemplifies this market-oriented logic. Based on individual capitalization with mandatory contributions managed by private pension fund administrators (AFPs), the system generates substantial disparities: those with higher lifetime earnings receive higher pension benefits, while workers with lower earnings face inadequate pensions (Bril-Mascarenhas & Maillet, 2019; Evans & Pienknagura, 2024). Currently, 11 million contributors participate in private pension fund administrators, yet 27% of the labor force remains outside the system due to informality (Superintendencia de Pensiones, 2024). This structure has produced high poverty rates among the elderly population, generating profound discontent and social movements demanding reform. Nevertheless, and paradoxically, recent studies show that a growing proportion of the population is willing to justify pension inequalities based on individual contributions (Castillo et al., 2025).

This paradox reflects broader transformations in Chilean class structure and moral orientations. Despite expansion of middle sectors and relative social fluidity, recent evidence points to growing rigidity in relative mobility, particularly following economic slowdown and the COVID-19 crisis (Barozet et al., 2021). Simultaneously, meritocratic beliefs have intensified, accompanied by internalization of market-based moral orientations where individuals and families position themselves as agents responsible for maximizing their market value (Canales Cerón et al., 2021; Mau, 2015). In this context of persistent inequality combined with strengthened meritocratic beliefs, it becomes crucial to examine how the justification of pension inequalities varies according to factors such as social class and meritocratic orientations, and how these justifications have evolved in recent years (Castillo et al., 2025; Castillo et al., 2024).
