Class

Despite recent criticism of class analysis, contemporary research has shown that class inequalities remain relevant in explaining perceptions of social justice (Dodson, 2017; Edlund & Lindh, 2015; Lindh & McCall, 2020; Wright, 2000). From a neo-Marxist perspectives, class divisions are defined by relations of ownership over productive resources and the patterns of exploitation inscribed in them, which generate differentiated economic positions (Wright, 2000). These class positions act as determinants of how material interests and shared life experiences structure individuals’ subjective perceptions of social reality and the justification of inequalities, including those present in systems such as pensions.

The material interests derived from class position constitute a fundamental bridge between the objective structure of inequality and subjective perceptions of justice and resource distribution (Bottero, 2004; Langsæther & Evans, 2020; Svallfors, 2006). According to different studies, the subordinate or privileged position that individuals occupy in the class structure generates material interests that are potentially antagonistic due to exploitation, producing significant variations in how they perceive and justify different social inequalities (Langsæther & Evans, 2020; Wright, 2005). In the context of pension systems based on individual contributions, such as the Chilean system, these differences in class interests are particularly relevant, as they define who benefits from the system and who remains in vulnerable positions.

Empirical research on the relationship between class position and perceptions of justice has shown a consistent pattern: perceptions of fairness tend to be structured according to class position (Edlund & Lindh, 2015; Pérez Ahumada, 2024). Individuals belonging to lower classes tend to perceive greater conflict and prefer greater redistribution, while those in upper classes tend to justify inequalities based on meritocratic criteria (Edlund & Lindh, 2015; Lindh & McCall, 2020). This pattern can be explained by the fact that individuals in privileged positions experience less impairment of their material interests and tend to perceive their own situation as the result of their effort and qualifications rather than exclusion and exploitation of other classes (Lindh & McCall, 2020).

In the specific case of Chile, the individually funded pension system has generated enormous disparities and vulnerability among the elderly population, creating a scenario in which the justification of these inequalities takes on particular importance (Borzutzky & Hyde, 2016; Bril-Mascarenhas & Maillet, 2019; Cabib, 2025; Evans & Pienknagura, 2024; Previsión Social, 2023). Despite documented social discontent, recent studies have identified that a significant proportion of the population is willing to justify pension inequalities based on meritocratic beliefs and notions of market justice (Castillo et al., 2025). This apparent contradiction suggests that beliefs about how pensions should be distributed do not necessarily align with the objective material interests of the classes, raising questions about how social class and meritocratic beliefs interact in the justification of inequalities.

Understanding how meritocratic beliefs and class position are associated with the justification of pension inequalities is crucial to understanding both the persistence of social discontent and the acceptance of highly unequal systems (Busemeyer & Iversen, 2020; Svallfors, 2006). Preferences for market justice in pensions represent a specific political expression of how individuals, depending on their class position, assess the legitimacy of a system that distributes resources in a differentiated manner. Analysing these associations over time allows us to identify how these justifications evolve and what factors explain why some groups maintain or modify their assessments of what constitutes a fair pension system.
